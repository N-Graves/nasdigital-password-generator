Why a number and not a strength meter

Weak/Medium/Strong meters mislead in both directions. A long dictionary phrase scores well on character variety while being trivially guessable, and a genuinely strong password can read as "medium" for happening to contain no symbol. The figure shown above is the actual one: how many bits of guessing the password is worth, given the alphabet it was drawn from.

The line beside it separates the two threats that differ by orders of magnitude. Guessing at a login form is slow and rate-limited. Cracking a leaked database of password hashes is neither, and it is the one that decides whether a password was long enough.

Every character is drawn evenly

Each character comes from the whole allowed alphabet with equal probability. Plenty of generators instead guarantee one character from each category and then shuffle, which sounds more thorough and is slightly worse: it skews the result and makes the bit count they print beside it a small lie. The trade here is that a short password may happen to contain no symbol — the tool says so when it does, and you can generate another.

Leaving out the lookalikes

0 and O, 1 and l and I are the ones people get wrong reading a password aloud or copying it off a screen. Leaving them out is not free — it shrinks the alphabet, so it costs a little entropy, and the figure above moves when you toggle it rather than pretending otherwise.

Nothing is stored, and nothing is sent

The password is generated in your browser and never leaves it. There is no history, no vault and no way to get one back once it is off the screen — deliberately, because adding any of that would turn a tool with nothing to protect into one that needs a security posture it was never designed for.
